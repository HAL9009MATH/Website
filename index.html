Jiayin Pan University of California, Santa Cruz

Nonnegative Ricci curvature and virtual abelianness in dimensions less than 12

Wednesday, October 7 · 4:00 PM · McHenry 4130

Abstract

For any complete Riemannian manifold \(M^n\) with nonnegative Ricci curvature and sublinear diameter growth, we establish a dimensional constraint \(n\ge 4s(s-1)+k+1\) if the fundamental group \(\pi_1(M)\) contains a torsion-free nilpotent subgroup of rank \(k\) and step \(s\ge 2\). As a consequence, if such a manifold \(M\) has dimension \(n<12\), then \(\pi_1(M)\) is almost abelian. This is a joint work with Dimitri Navarro and Xingyu Zhu.

Oct 14 2026

Max Su University of California, Santa Cruz

Global existence of an initial boundary value problem in 2+1 dimensions for the Einstein equation via a Hamiltonian approach

Wednesday, October 14 · 4:00 PM · McHenry 4130

Abstract

The Einstein equation describes our universe elegantly. One fundamental aspect is to understand the dynamics of the solutions. In this talk, we use a finite dimensional Hamiltonian system to describe solutions to the initial boundary value problem (IBVP) for the Einstein equation in 2+1 dimensions with totally geodesic timelike boundary. By analyzing the flows in the Hamitonian system, we can prove the longtime existence of the IBVP. This work shows more possibilities of simplifying the analysis of the Einstein equation geometrically.

Oct 21 2026

Matthew Kvalheim University of Maryland

On the universality of linear dynamics

Wednesday, October 21 · 4:00 PM · McHenry 4130

Abstract

Which nonlinear dynamical systems can be embedded as invariant subsets of linear systems of greater dimension? This is a natural variant of a question posed by Tao regarding the universality of potential well dynamics, where the class of potential well systems is replaced by linear systems. It is also an important question for numerical algorithms associated with "applied Koopman operator theory" that seek to model the evolution of observations of an unknown nonlinear system by a linear system on the observation space. In this talk, I will first describe a generalization of Tao's universality problem following Cardona–Presas and González-Prieto–Miranda–Peralta-Salas. I will then specialize to the linear universality problem and present necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of linearizing embeddings for a broad class of nonlinear systems. Along the way, I will also present various consequences and examples, including strong counterexamples to the oft-repeated claim that nonlinear systems with multiple isolated equilibria do not admit linearizing embeddings. Aspects of this talk are based on joint works with Philip Arathoon and with Eduardo D. Sontag.

Short Bio

Matthew D. Kvalheim is currently an Assistant Professor of Mathematics at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. His research draws on differential topology and other geometric-topological methods to study nonlinear systems. In 2026 he received the Young Investigator Program Award from the Air Force Office of Scientific Research and the Early Career Faculty Excellence Award from UMBC. He is on the editorial board of Mathematics of Control, Signals, and Systems. Previously, he held postdoctoral appointments at the University of Pennsylvania and the University of Michigan, where he also obtained his Ph.D. and M.S. degrees.

Oct 28 2026

Therese Landry University of California, Santa Barbara

Hack's Law, Erosion, and Optimal Transport: Developing Mathematical Models and Computational Methods

Wednesday, October 28 · 4:00 PM · McHenry 4130

Abstract

Hack's Law says that the length of the main river in a river basin scales with the area of the river basin to the power 0.58, thereby prompting speculation on whether there exist optimality principles governing natural processes like channel formation. Identification of such principles and their continued mathematical development could lead to the formulation of a theoretical framework which supports study not only of fluvial landscape evolution, but other geomorphological processes as well. Erosion is nature's process of “moving dirt.” In 1781, Monge initiated the theory of optimal transport with the question, “Given a pile of sand and a pit of equal volume, how can one optimally transport the sand into the pit?” Building on earlier work by Birnir, Merchant, Smith, Cattan, and Rowlett, we aim to extend optimal transport theory to develop mathematical models and computational methods for studying erosion.

Via optimal transport theory, we prove the existence of unique global weak solutions to equations describing the sediment flow in the evolution of fluvial land surfaces, with constant water depth. While an earlier existence theory result by Birnir and Landry demonstrates that the slopes, or gradients of the surface, are moved optimally, our current work proves that erosion does what Monge proposed: move sediment optimally from the mountainside to the river. This is joint work with Bjorn Birnir.

Matthias Wink University of California, Santa Barbara

Vanishing theorems for Kaehler manifolds

Friday, November 20 · 3:00 PM · McHenry 4130

Abstract

A celebrated result of Mori and Sui-Yau says that manifolds with positive bisectional curvature are biholomorphic to complex projective space. In this talk we will introduce new curvature conditions that provide characterizations of cohomology complex projective spaces. More generally, we will discuss vanishing results for harmonic (p,q)-forms based on conditions on the Kaehler curvature operator or the Calabi curvature operator. This talk is based on several joint works, with K. Broder, X. Li, J. Nienhaus, P. Petersen, F. Ricci and J. Stanfield.

Dec 2 2026

Yusen Xia University of California, Santa Barbara

Neumann eigenvalues and hot spots on geodesic triangles

Wednesday, December 2 · 4:00 PM · McHenry 4130

Abstract

How small can the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue of a triangle be when its diameter is fixed? I will discuss a sharp answer for geodesic triangles in two-dimensional space forms, extending a Euclidean result of Laugesen and Siudeja. In the spherical case, we assume the diameter is at most \(\pi/2\), and new equality phenomena occur at the endpoint.

The proof combines eigenvalue comparison with properties of nodal sets, giving a unified treatment of positive, zero, and negative curvature. I will also discuss a hot-spots theorem for non-acute spherical triangles: apart from a special equality family, the maximum and minimum of the first nonconstant Neumann eigenfunction occur at the endpoints of the longest side.

This is joint work with Shoo Seto and Guofang Wei.
